# Chapter 1: Your Network Is Already There

You may already know the person who can help you build the next thing.

You may have known them for years.

The difficulty is that you know them as your cousin, your old teammate, your auntie's friend, the person who always sorts out the event. You know who to call when you need a ride. You know who can cook for thirty people. You know who will tell you the truth when your idea has too much mouth and no legs.

But have you ever asked what those same people know how to build?

Who can explain a complicated process? Who keeps a business running when the owner is away? Who knows what customers ask every day? Who understands the language people actually use when the official form makes no sense?

That knowledge deserves a place in the room.

Diaspora empowerment begins when we take the people, skills and relationships around us seriously enough to connect them with purpose.

## The conversation after the conversation

Consider this illustrative scene.

A family call is winding down. Someone is washing dishes while the phone leans against a cup. Someone else is trying to hear over the television. A relative back home mentions that the same customer questions keep filling up their messages: opening hours, directions, what is available, whether an order is ready.

A younger relative abroad knows how to organize information and make a clear, readable document. They have been doing it at work for months.

The call moves on.

The person running the business goes back to answering the same questions. The person with the skill goes back to using it somewhere else.

Nobody has failed. Nobody has deliberately withheld help. The connection simply has not been made.

There is a possible Major Move inside that ordinary conversation: ask whether a short, easy-to-share information sheet would help. Listen to the answer. If the answer is yes, agree what belongs on it, make a first version, and let the person who knows the business check it.

No promise of a new company. No claim that one document will transform an economy. One useful piece of work, made with the person who needs it.

That is enough to begin.

The size of the first contribution does not determine its dignity. What matters is whether it is wanted, useful and possible to sustain.

## You can belong in more than one place

The word *diaspora* can sound like something reserved for conferences and reports. Here, we are speaking about people connected to a homeland while living elsewhere, and the relationships that continue across those places.

That connection can be close or complicated. It can involve language, family, memory, food, music, responsibility, curiosity or the wish to understand a history you did not get to learn properly.

You do not have to perform a particular version of Caribbean identity to take part in this work. You do not have to pretend every island has the same experience. And living abroad does not make your relationship with home simple.

You might feel settled where you are and still miss something. You might feel connected to your roots and still disagree with expectations placed on you. You might want to contribute while being tired of being treated as if you have unlimited money, unlimited time or an answer for everything.

Those tensions belong in an honest conversation about empowerment.

A useful relationship makes room for what each person can actually give. Sometimes that is an hour of teaching. Sometimes it is a practical introduction. Sometimes it is saying, clearly and respectfully, that you cannot take something on this month.

Belonging does not require exhaustion.

The question for this book is practical: how can the knowledge you carry become useful across the places and people you care about?

## From brain drain to brain circulation

The existing MAIM writing returns to a central idea: **brain circulation**.

When people leave a place, something can be lost. A family may miss daily support. An organization may lose experience. A community may struggle to replace a skilled person. We do not need to deny those costs to see further possibilities.

The person who leaves can also develop skills, relationships and perspectives that travel back through a willing exchange.

An explanation travels. A workshop travels. A useful template travels. An introduction can connect someone to a person they would otherwise never meet.

Physical return is one possible route. It is not the only route.

In this book, brain circulation means creating a deliberate path for knowledge and opportunity to move between people and places. The exchange should respect the people on both sides. Someone living abroad can contribute experience; someone working locally can explain what the problem really is, what has already been tried and what would fit daily life.

That second contribution is essential.

Before you design the answer, listen to the person who lives with the question.

If your proposed tool requires a connection they cannot rely on, it needs another design. If the instructions use language nobody would naturally use, they need another draft. If the idea creates more work than it removes, the person doing that work should be able to say so.

Circulation includes correction. You bring what you know, and you remain willing to learn.

## A contact list becomes a working relationship

A name in your phone is a possibility. A working relationship needs a little more shape.

You need to understand what a person does, what they care about and whether they want to participate. You need a useful reason to connect. You need to follow through on what you agreed.

Start by looking for forms of ability you may have overlooked.

The person who coordinates transport knows how to plan around changing conditions. The person who keeps an event calm knows something about people, timing and communication. A tradesperson understands where instructions break down in practice. An elder may remember how a community solved a problem before anybody thought to describe it as a system.

You do not need to rename these people as technology experts to respect their expertise.

You do need to ask how that expertise could help define the work.

Suppose you can produce a polished digital guide. The person who answers customer questions knows what the guide should say. The person who reads it on a small phone can tell you whether it is usable. The elder whose story appears in it decides what may be shared.

A strong contribution can involve several kinds of knowledge without pretending that every contribution is identical.

The first step is to make those contributions visible and agree how they fit together.

## What sovereignty means here

The MAIM framework calls for sovereign communities of creators, not consumers.

In these pages, sovereignty is a practical direction: greater ability to make decisions about your work, understand the systems you depend on, and retain meaningful control over what you create. This book does not use the word to confer a legal status.

You can begin with ordinary questions.

Do you have a copy of your work? Can you revise it without asking someone else? Do you understand who can access it? Can another trusted person continue the task if you are unavailable? Do the people who contributed understand how their work will be used?

These questions bring the vision down to something you can act on.

A community project might use a commercial tool and still take sensible steps toward greater control: keep usable copies, document the process, understand costs, and train more than one person to maintain it.

The aim is to build judgment as well as an output.

If you create a helpful guide but nobody else can update it, the project still depends on you. If you show someone how to maintain it, leave clear instructions and agree what happens next, you have created more room for them to act.

That is a meaningful form of progress.

## Start with a need somebody recognizes

It is tempting to begin with the technology.

You discover a new tool. You see what it can produce. Immediately, everything looks like an opportunity to use it.

Slow down long enough to ask what would help.

A person may need clearer instructions, a simpler booking process, a better way to organize existing information, or a chance to learn something they have wanted to understand for years. Their answer may require very little technology.

Artificial intelligence can help prepare drafts or organize material. Its output still needs checking by someone who understands the subject and the people involved. Sometimes a conversation and a page of careful instructions are the right first tools.

You can learn more about a problem by helping with one small part of it than by designing a large platform around assumptions.

Ask what is frustrating. Ask how the person currently handles it. Ask what they would keep about the existing way of working. That last question matters: a process can contain something valuable even when parts of it are difficult.

Then ask whether they want your help with a specific piece.

An invitation leaves room for a real answer. The person can accept, suggest something else or decline.

## Your private diaspora connection map

Take a blank page. Keep this exercise private unless the people involved have agreed otherwise. You are mapping relationships to understand where you might contribute, not assembling a list to market to.

Write down five people connected to a community you care about. First names or private initials are enough. You can include people living locally, abroad or between places. If five feels like too many, begin with one.

For each person, consider four questions:

1. What do I actually know they can do?
2. What need or difficulty have they described themselves?
3. What could I offer without overpromising?
4. What would I need to ask before taking action?

Separate what you know from what you assume. If you do not know the person's current work or needs, write *ask*. A gap in your knowledge is useful information.

Now add yourself to the page.

What can you explain, make, organize, repair, teach or connect? Which of those skills can you use confidently today? Where would you need support? How much time can you realistically offer?

Look for one intersection between a need somebody has named and a contribution you can sustain.

Keep it small enough to finish. A clear document. A short lesson. A useful introduction made with consent. A tested first version of something the other person can assess.

The exercise is complete when you have a better question to ask a real person. You do not need a grand announcement.

## Your first Major Move

Choose one person from your map.

Use your own words to tell them what you noticed and ask whether a specific contribution would help. For example, you could explain that you know how to organize the information they mentioned, and offer to work through a first version together.

That is a suggested approach, not a script you must copy.

If they are interested, agree on three things: the small output, the time each person can give, and how you will know whether the result is useful. If the work involves someone else's information, image or story, settle permission before using it.

If they decline, respect the answer. You have learned something about the fit. You can keep the relationship without turning it into a project.

Your first Major Move is to begin one willing, useful exchange.

A skill can stay unused because nobody knows it is there. A need can stay unresolved because nobody has asked carefully enough. Your first bridge may be the conversation that brings the two together.

You do not need everybody on board to have that conversation.

Start with one person. Listen well. Make a contribution you can stand behind.
