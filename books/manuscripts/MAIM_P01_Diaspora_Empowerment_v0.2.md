## Pillar 01 of The 10 Pillars of the Major AI Mindset

By Major Dream Williams

**Complete six-chapter working draft · v0.2 · 26 October 2026**

*Building sovereign communities of creators, not consumers*

## Before we begin

This book is an invitation to recognize what you carry and put it to work with people you care about. By the end, you will have a practical plan for one useful exchange: a skill shared, a problem worked through, an introduction made or a small project tested together.

Start where you are. You do not need to run a company, know how to code or live in the country your family comes from. You need something you can contribute, somebody willing to work with you and enough honesty to learn what the situation requires.

Read with a notebook. The exercises build on each other, but this is not a race. Keep personal contact details in your own notes. Examples marked hypothetical are teaching illustrations, not accounts of clients or projects that happened.

The first-person passages are edited from my supplied reflections and recording. They express my account and perspective; they are not a verbatim transcript. Other authors' ideas are credited in the text and source notes. The practical methods developed here are MAIM teaching adaptations unless otherwise identified.

# Chapter 1: I Am One of Them

I want to prove that I was here.

That is part of why I want to write these books. I want to get my thoughts out and show how mindset can move a person from one place to somewhere else. Another part of the same city. Another country. Another way of seeing what is possible.

For a long time, people could see the physical things I could do. Lift something. Push something. Defend something or someone. Be the mouthpiece. Sell the idea. Hold it down.

There were other gifts I had to learn to recognize.

I asked questions. Then I asked another question. I wanted to pick up the signal and remove the noise. That could get me into trouble growing up, because I kept examining things I was expected to accept.

As I got older, I looked more closely at my family and my culture. I started to recognize the collective minds around me, contributing in ways that were similar but different.

I am Jamaican. You might recognize that in how I speak, what I eat, what I cook or how I explain something. Those things travelled with me. They became part of the skills and tools I took into the world.

I did not understand all their value straight away. I understood more when I met people from similar backgrounds and recognized what we carried in common.

The more people looked at me as somebody special, the more I looked back.

There were people who started where I started. People who played basketball. People from the islands. People from the South Bronx. My path took different turns. I made choices and pivots. Good people contributed along the way. I also had opportunities and freedom that not everybody gets.

When I go back and see people from the old neighborhood or people from the rock, I let them know: I am one of them.

I made different choices. I am still one of them.

I remember travelling down to Manhattan by myself and seeing something different. I could look at what somebody else was doing and think: if you can do it, I can do it.

I carried that curiosity. I kept a nimble mind. Eventually I wanted to find out how what I had learned would work somewhere else in the world.

Now I think about family and legacy. I think about the people who are no longer here and the fact that I am still here. I wake up thankful that I have another opportunity to do something. I want what I have learned to be useful beyond my own life.

## Recognize the whole person

When I look at the Caribbean, I see distinct islands, histories and personalities. Jamaica matters deeply to me, but I do not need to make every island the same to recognize our connections.

I also do not accept a picture of our people that starts and ends with what was done to them.

Vincent Brown's work on *Tacky's Revolt* helps widen that picture. In a Harvard interview, he explains how soldiers captured in Gold Coast conflicts were sold across the Atlantic, where shared language and religious ties could help them regroup and resist. His account places Jamaican revolt within a wider history of warfare and political struggle.[1]

The lesson I draw is to look for the knowledge and capacity a label conceals. A person subjected to enslavement had a life, relationships, abilities and intentions. Recognizing those things changes the questions we ask about the past and the attention we give people now.

What knowledge did your family carry? What did people learn to make, organize, grow, repair, negotiate or protect? Which of those abilities survived through practice, even when nobody wrote them into a curriculum?

You can investigate these questions through conversations, records and reading. Different sources will give you different parts of the picture. Listen closely enough to notice where they meet and where they differ.

## What you carry counts

Perhaps you translate between generations. Perhaps you understand how a small business actually runs when the official process stops working. Perhaps you can explain something complicated without making the listener feel small.

These are useful abilities. Name them precisely enough that you can develop them.

Being good at getting people together might mean you know how to invite, schedule, mediate and follow up. Being good with food might include sourcing, portioning, timing, hospitality and teaching. Being the person everybody calls can mean you hold valuable knowledge. It can also mean you need stronger boundaries.

Recognition gives you a starting point. Practice and feedback help turn it into dependable work.

In the recovered MAIM writing, diaspora empowerment includes cultural connection, bridge building and the creation of economic ecosystems. Begin with connection. You cannot responsibly build the larger structure while ignoring the people who will live with it.

## Belonging in more than one place

For this book, diaspora describes people living away from a homeland and the relationships that continue across those places and generations. Those relationships can be close, distant, joyful or complicated.

You may love where you live and still feel a pull toward your roots. You may know little about your family's place of origin and want to learn. You may be tired of being treated as if living abroad means having unlimited money.

There is room for all of that here.

Contribution does not require physical return. Nor does it require accepting every request. A healthy exchange makes room for what people can give and what they need to keep for themselves.

You are allowed to learn about your roots without pretending expertise. You are allowed to bring a skill without claiming to understand every local problem. You are allowed to receive knowledge from the people you hoped to help.

## Exercise: what travelled with you?

Make three columns in your notebook: **What I learned**, **Where I use it**, and **Where else it might help**.

Write five entries. Include abilities learned at home, through work, through sport or through necessity. For each one, describe something you can actually do. Replace a broad word like leadership with a concrete action, such as helping a group agree on its next task.

Circle one ability you can offer confidently today. Underline one you want to strengthen. Beside each, name the person who could give you honest feedback.

If your first answer is that you have nothing useful, ask yourself what people already seek your help with. That may reveal something you have been overlooking.

## Major Move

Have one conversation about a skill, practice or story that matters to your community. Ask where it came from and how it is used. Listen before deciding what it could become. Ask permission before recording or sharing anything.

**Carry forward:** One ability you recognize, one question you want to investigate and one person whose knowledge you want to understand better.

# Chapter 2: Go Left: From Consumer to Creator

Everybody can point you toward the same destination. The education is there. The incentives are there. The promise of money and power is there. After a while, you can start believing that everything valuable must be over there too.

What did you walk past on the left?

Your people's knowledge. Their relationships. Their ways of making something work when resources were limited. Things you grew up around that you never thought to call valuable because nobody handed you a certificate for knowing them.

In my humble opinion, go left. There are treasures in places people have not bothered to look.

That is how I think about opportunity. I want the confidence to examine a different route and the discipline to find out whether it leads somewhere useful.

## An influence, and my application

There is a connection here with *Blue Ocean Strategy* by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne. Their approach seeks new market space through value innovation: improving value for buyers while pursuing lower costs. It asks a business to rethink what it offers, rather than accept the existing basis of competition.[2]

My application begins with the value we overlook in our own communities. Which needs have been treated as too small to matter? What ability do we already have that could address them? Who has been left out of the way a service is designed?

A different direction still needs a destination. Being unusual does not tell you whether your work helps somebody. You have to ask, try and learn.

A crowded market can contain an overlooked need. An empty market can be empty because the proposed service is difficult, unwanted or unaffordable. Your job is to investigate the difference.

## What creating means

You do not have to stop consuming to become a creator. You will still read books, buy services and use other people's tools. The shift is to take an active role in making something useful and understanding the terms on which you make it.

You might create a clear instruction sheet, a reliable lesson, a repair service, a story archive or a better way to coordinate an existing activity.

Ask what remains after you finish. Is there something another person can use, maintain or learn from? Have you strengthened an ability, or only produced an object that depends entirely on your presence?

A business owner may need a page of accurate answers more than a sophisticated chatbot. A young person may need someone to explain how to practice a skill. An elder may want a story preserved for the family and kept away from public platforms.

Those are different needs. Take each seriously on its own terms.

## Look at the system around the work

One reason history matters to this book is that wealth moves through systems. The person doing the visible work is not always the person receiving the largest benefit.

Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* argues that wealth accumulated through slavery helped finance Britain's industrial development. That argument asks us to examine the connections among coerced labor, commerce and capital, rather than treating the plantation as an isolated enterprise.[3]

Insurance belongs in that history too. Lloyd's acknowledges that its market participated in, and benefited significantly from, the transatlantic slave trade and the goods produced through enslavement.[4]

My present-day question is: when we create value, where does it go?

You can ask that about a small project. Who does the work? Who pays? Who receives the revenue? Who owns the account? Who can continue using the material? Who is left carrying the costs?

You do not need to become a financial historian to start noticing these relationships. You do need to understand the arrangements you enter.

## Sovereignty in everyday decisions

The MAIM framework calls for sovereign communities of creators, not consumers. Here, sovereignty means moving toward greater practical control over our work and choices. It does not confer a legal status.

Start with questions you can answer:

- Do we have usable copies of what we created?
- Can we update them without depending on one person?
- Do contributors understand how their work will be used?
- Do we know the recurring costs?
- Can we move the work if a tool stops meeting our needs?

Using a commercial platform can be a sensible decision. Understand what you are depending on and what you can retain. The platform should serve the work, and the people doing the work should understand the dependency.

Owning a file is one form of control. Having the knowledge to maintain it is another. A community can accumulate files and still be unable to act if nobody understands how they fit together.

Teach the process as you build the output.

## Exercise: follow one piece of value

Choose something you already make or help deliver. Draw its path from the person who contributes the first input to the person who uses the result.

At each step, write what is added, what it costs and who makes the decision. Include the less visible work: explaining, organizing, translating, checking and maintaining.

Now identify one place where your contribution disappears from view, one dependency you need to understand and one improvement you can make yourself.

This is an observation exercise. Do not assume every fee is exploitation or every outside tool is harmful. Examine what is exchanged and whether the arrangement serves the people involved.

## Major Move

Create or improve one useful thing that its intended user can retain. Explain how to use it and how to change it. Ask the user to show you what they can do with it without your help.

That demonstration will tell you more than a compliment.

**Carry forward:** A useful output, a clearer understanding of who benefits and one step toward greater control.

# Chapter 3: Put Brain Circulation to Work

The recovered MAIM writing reframes brain drain as brain circulation. I want us to see a person who moves as somebody who can widen a network of knowledge and opportunity.

Migration can still leave real gaps. Families lose daily support. Workplaces lose experienced people. Recognizing circulation means asking how we can build willing connections across distance, while taking those losses seriously.

A skill can travel through a lesson. A relationship can travel through an introduction. A practical method can travel through a clear document and a person who understands it. Physical return is one route among several.

The exchange should move in both directions. Someone abroad brings experience. Someone working locally brings knowledge of the actual conditions. Both should have influence over what happens.

## Begin with the people already there

A contact list shows who you can reach. It does not show who wants to collaborate, what they need or what time they can give.

Start with five people, or one if that is where you are. Include people at home and abroad. For each, write what you know they can do, what difficulty they have described and what you still need to ask.

Be careful with the phrase my community. Which people do you mean? A group of market vendors? An association of former teammates? Parents supporting an after-school activity? Naming the group helps you listen to actual people instead of a broad idea of belonging.

You may share a background and still misunderstand their day. Ask how the work happens now. Ask what they have already tried. Ask what they want to preserve about the current approach.

That last question can save you from removing something valuable in the name of improvement.

## A small exchange with a clear shape

Use six actions to organize your first contribution: **ask, agree, contribute, review, document and follow up**. This is a practical synthesis for this book, built from the recovered MAIM exercises.

**Ask.** Understand one problem in the person's own words. Find out whether help is wanted. Keep your first conversation open enough for them to suggest a different problem.

**Agree.** Describe the small output, who will do what and when you will look at it together. State your limits. A two-hour contribution should not quietly become an open-ended support role.

**Contribute.** Make the smallest useful version. Use information the participant has agreed to share. If the task grows, pause and agree on the new scope before continuing.

**Review.** Watch somebody use the result. Ask what was useful, confusing or missing. Make space for an answer that disappoints you.

**Document.** Leave enough information for the next person to understand what was made, what remains uncertain and how to update it. Documentation can be a page, a demonstration or both.

**Follow up.** Return at the time you agreed. Decide together whether to continue, revise, hand over or stop.

You can use this sequence for a lesson, a tool, an introduction or a small service. The amount of formality should fit the work.

## A working example

*Hypothetical illustration: the people and events below are invented for teaching.*

Imagine a food-business owner in Jamaica and a relative abroad who knows how to organize information. The owner spends time answering recurring questions about collection times and how to place an order. The relative offers to help.

Their first idea is a chatbot. During the conversation, the owner explains that opening hours change, the menu is short and a pinned message is easier to maintain.

They agree to make one customer information sheet. The owner supplies the current details. The relative arranges them clearly. The owner checks the language and decides what customers should see.

The contribution draws on both kinds of expertise. Knowing how to produce a document helps. Knowing the business determines whether the document is right.

At the review, they should ask whether the sheet answers the real questions and whether the owner can update it. They should also ask what would happen when the relative is unavailable.

The example has no promised revenue result. The first test concerns usefulness and maintainability. Those are meaningful things to learn.

## Reciprocity without a hidden debt

You can contribute generously and still discuss costs. You can receive payment and still create community value. What damages trust is leaving the arrangement unclear.

Explain whether you are volunteering, exchanging skills or providing a paid service. A gift should not become an unannounced invoice. An unpaid trial should not quietly turn into permanent free labor.

Reciprocity can mean payment, teaching, credit, a useful introduction or helping maintain the result. Agree what applies rather than assuming everyone understands the same bargain.

People also need room to decline. A relationship can remain valuable even when it does not become a project.

## Exercise: write your exchange agreement

Complete these sentences in plain language:

- The person or group I want to work with is…
- The need they have described is…
- The small contribution we could make is…
- They would contribute… and I would contribute…
- The time and cost we can manage are…
- We will review usefulness by…
- We will stop or reconsider if…

Mark any answer you have not discussed as an assumption. Take those assumptions into the conversation. Do not present your private worksheet as an agreement somebody else has already accepted.

## Major Move

Invite one willing person to shape a small exchange with you. Agree on the output and review time. Leave with a specific next action that each of you understands.

**Carry forward:** One collaboration with a clear purpose, realistic limits and a chance for both people to learn.

# Chapter 4: Build One Bridge, Then Test It

A bridge has to meet the ground on both sides.

You can have an exciting idea and still miss the conditions where somebody will use it. You can understand a community well and still need help with the technical work. Bring those forms of knowledge together early.

The recovered Comprehensive Legacy Edition includes a five-step Cultural-Tech Bridge exercise. We will use its sequence here: cultural assets, technical skills, intersections, community validation and the simplest useful test. The point is to make a connection you can examine in practice.

## Step 1: identify a cultural asset

An asset might be a language you speak, a practice you understand, a relationship of trust or knowledge of a recurring difficulty.

Be specific. Caribbean culture is too broad to design from. Understanding how a particular group communicates about an event gives you something you can investigate.

Also ask whether the knowledge is yours to use. Knowing a story does not necessarily mean you may publish it. Being welcomed into a community does not give you authority to speak for everyone.

Write one sentence describing the knowledge you bring and another describing the limits of your understanding.

## Step 2: assess your practical skills

List what you can do reliably now. Include organizing, interviewing, editing, designing, teaching and making things work with limited resources. Add technical skills where you have them.

Separate ability from interest. Being curious about building an app is a good reason to learn. It is not a reason to promise one next week.

Identify the task you can finish yourself and the task where you need a collaborator. That distinction helps you make a credible offer.

## Step 3: find an intersection

Look for a connection between what you understand and what you can do.

In our hypothetical food-business example, the intersection is familiarity with customer questions and the ability to organize clear information. The useful result may fit on one page.

Another possible intersection is a trusted elder's willingness to teach and a younger person's ability to prepare a family listening session. The result might be a conversation with agreed boundaries, without a recording or public upload.

An intersection becomes a project when someone wants the result and the people involved can make it happen. Until then, treat it as a possibility.

## Step 4: listen for evidence of a need

Ask about the most recent time the difficulty occurred. What happened? What did the person do? What was frustrating? What worked well enough to keep?

These questions give you more useful information than asking whether somebody likes your idea. People may encourage you because they care about you. They may imagine a different product from the one you intend to build.

Speak with enough relevant people to challenge your assumptions. The earlier source suggests ten conversations; use that as an invitation to listen more widely, not as a magic threshold. Ten friendly answers do not prove demand.

Look for differences as well as patterns. A tool that helps one business may create work for another. A person who prefers a voice message may not want a form. Record what you learned without keeping unnecessary personal details.

Then choose a problem narrow enough to test.

## Step 5: make the simplest useful version

For the food-business example, begin with the actual information customers need. Make it readable on the phone the owner uses. Let the owner check every detail. Put the editable version somewhere the owner can access.

Artificial intelligence could help arrange a draft or suggest clearer wording. The owner still needs to check the facts and voice. Use approved business information; customer messages do not need to become input to an outside service.

A tool's ability to generate more material does not mean you need more material. The right version may be shorter than your first attempt.

If the owner cannot maintain it, improve the handover or simplify the design. If customers cannot find the information, change where it appears. If the problem turns out to be something else, reconsider the project.

## Decide what the test will tell you

Before trying the output, choose a question you can answer. Can a customer find the collection time? Can the owner change the menu without help? Is an important question still missing?

Ask a willing person to attempt the task with a sample question. Watch without explaining every step. Notice where they hesitate. Their difficulty is useful design information.

Afterwards, ask what they expected and what they would change. Record the result plainly: what was attempted, what happened and what you changed.

A small test cannot establish that you have transformed a community. It can show that a particular version helped with a particular task. That is enough evidence to guide the next version.

## Exercise: your bridge card

Write a one-page card with six entries: the cultural knowledge, the practical skill, the named need, the person helping define it, the first output and the test question.

Underneath, write what would make you stop. Perhaps the participant does not want the work, the cost is too high or the project requires information you should not collect.

A stopping condition helps you protect your time and the relationship. You can keep the learning even when you set the idea aside.

## Major Move

Make one small version and ask an intended user to try it. Record one thing that worked and one thing you changed because of their feedback.

**Carry forward:** Evidence from use, rather than confidence built only on the idea.

# Chapter 5: Protect the People and the Work

If we say empowerment, the people involved should have more room to understand, decide and act. Examine the arrangement as carefully as the output.

The recovered MAIM source warns against extraction and cultural tokenism. Those warnings become practical when you ask who contributes, who decides, who benefits and who can leave.

You can mean well and still make an arrangement that depends on somebody else's unpaid effort. You can celebrate a community publicly while leaving its members out of the decisions. Notice these possibilities early enough to change the work.

## Permission has a purpose

An elder might agree to tell you a story and want it kept within the family. A business owner might let you read a customer message to understand a problem and still object to its use in a promotional example.

Explain the use you intend. Ask whether recording is acceptable, who may see the result and whether it may be shared publicly. If you later want a different use, return to the person rather than treating the first permission as unlimited.

When documenting cultural knowledge, ask who else should be involved in the decision. Some material has a significance beyond the person you happen to interview.

For this book's exercises, collect less. You usually do not need identity documents, private financial records, health details or a complete contact list to test a simple idea. Use invented sample data where the real information adds no necessary value.

## Credit the thinking

People contribute in different ways. Someone may identify the real problem, supply the language, make an introduction, check the result or maintain it after the first enthusiasm fades.

Make those contributions visible. Ask how people want to be credited, including whether they prefer privacy. Agree before using their name or image in promotion.

The same care applies to books and other influences. You can explain a source's idea in your own words, identify the author and title, and show what you make of it. Keep quotation marks for actual wording. A citation does not turn copied passages into your own writing.

That is why this book names Brown, Williams, Kim and Mauborgne. Their contributions deserve credit. The connection to MAIM's practical teaching is an interpretation developed here.

You can acknowledge influence without surrendering your own perspective.

## Discuss control before success makes it complicated

A small project can become valuable. Decide early who keeps the files, who may revise them, who controls access and whether any participant may reuse the material elsewhere.

If money enters the arrangement, make the terms understandable. State what is paid, what is included and what requires another agreement. Where a formal ownership or commercial arrangement is needed, get appropriate help to put it in writing.

For an ordinary shared guide, a plain record of responsibilities may be enough to organize the work. For a business, an investment or valuable intellectual property, a friendly conversation may leave important questions unresolved. Match the agreement to the stakes.

Do not use family connection as a substitute for clarity. Clear expectations can help preserve the relationship.

## Make a handover somebody can use

Return to our hypothetical business information sheet. Suppose the relative creates a polished version but keeps the only editable copy in a personal account. Every update now requires the relative's availability.

A better handover gives the owner access to the working copy, explains how to update it and shows where the version customers see comes from. The owner tries the update while help is available.

Record recurring costs and what happens if the tool is no longer used. Do not pass passwords around as a shortcut; use the tool's supported access arrangements and keep account recovery under the appropriate person's control.

The handover should include permission to ask a question and an honest limit on future support. If you can help once a month, say so. If this is a one-time contribution, make that clear too.

## Leave room to stop

Sometimes the project does not fit. The need changes. A participant loses time. A tool adds complexity. The work may be worth pausing even when everybody likes each other.

Agree what happens to files and access if you stop. Return the materials people need. Remove access that is no longer appropriate. Discuss what can be retained and what should be deleted.

An orderly ending is part of responsible work. It protects the possibility of future collaboration.

## Exercise: the people-and-work check

Review your project with the participant. Answer these questions together:

1. What have we agreed to make, and what is outside the scope?
2. What information are we using, and for what purpose?
3. Who can see, change and share the result?
4. How will contributions be credited?
5. Who pays any costs and maintains the work?
6. What happens if either of us stops?

Write unresolved points beside the person who will address them. Resolve the relevant questions before expanding the use, audience or cost of the project.

## Major Move

Complete a handover or expectations conversation. Ask the other person to explain the arrangement back in their own words. Correct anything that you understood differently.

**Carry forward:** A contribution people can use with clear expectations and meaningful choice.

# Chapter 6: Make Circulation a Habit

I do not want this book to end as something you agreed with and put away.

I want you to look at what you carry and make a useful connection with it. Something small enough to do, clear enough to understand and honest enough to improve.

The recovered MAIM exercises ask readers to map their networks, share knowledge and transfer a skill. Bring those ideas together around one completed exchange. You can grow from there.

## Give the first week a purpose

Use this seven-day sequence as a flexible starting plan. It is a teaching proposal, not a requirement to finish on a particular schedule. If a conversation needs more time, take it.

**Day 1: recognize.** Complete your skills inventory. Name one ability you have been treating as ordinary and one community you want to understand better.

**Day 2: listen.** Speak with a willing person. Ask about a recent difficulty, what they already do and whether a small contribution would help.

**Day 3: agree.** Choose the output, responsibilities and limits. Write down the review question. Keep the work small enough for the time available.

**Day 4: make.** Build a first version from information the participant has agreed to use. Leave room for correction.

**Day 5: test.** Let the intended user try it. Observe a real task or a suitable sample task. Record what happened without inflating the result.

**Day 6: improve and hand over.** Make the important change. Give the participant the access and instructions needed to maintain the result. Confirm credit and any sharing boundaries.

**Day 7: review.** Decide whether to continue, revise, transfer responsibility or stop. Write what you learned and what you would do differently.

If you reach the end of the week with a clearer problem and no output, say that. You may need to reduce the scope. Honest learning gives you a better next step than claiming completion.

## Keep a record worth returning to

Use four short entries: **what we intended, what we did, what happened and what comes next**.

For a lesson, the evidence might be whether the learner can perform the task afterwards. For a guide, it might be whether someone can find the answer. For an introduction, it might simply be that both parties consented, connected and decided their own next step.

A record helps you distinguish activity from benefit. Sending a document is an action. Finding out whether it helped takes another conversation.

You do not need to turn every contribution into a public story. Keep private work private. When sharing a result, describe only what the evidence supports and what participants have agreed may be shared.

## Review after thirty days

Return to the person and the work. Is the result still useful? Can they maintain it? Did costs appear? Did you keep your promise? Did somebody else take the knowledge further?

Choose the next step together. A useful project may need a second contributor. A lesson may need clearer instructions. A task that remains burdensome may need a simpler approach.

Growth should follow usefulness and capacity. More participants, more features and more public attention all create responsibilities. Add them deliberately.

The historical MAIM material also includes a longer 30–60–90 roadmap. You can use longer horizons once the first exchange gives you something real to plan around. A calendar can organize progress; it cannot substitute for it.

## Each one teach one

One useful contribution can become a habit when somebody else learns how to carry it forward.

Invite the learner to explain the process. Let them adapt it to their circumstances. If they want to teach another person, help them do so without requiring every decision to pass through you.

In the original Scroll exercise, the Major Move was to reach three people with a useful tool, insight or opportunity. Keep the spirit of that invitation. Choose relevance over volume. One thoughtful exchange is a stronger beginning than three unwanted messages.

You can offer a skill, ask for one or connect people who want the introduction. Circulation includes receiving. You do not have to be the expert in every room to contribute to the network.

## Where the other teachings fit

The separate ABC pathway—Awareness, Belief and Context—can help a reader recognize a starting point. Pillar 01 asks you to put that recognition into a willing exchange across your relationships.

Community & Collaboration, Pillar 05, offers a place to develop the collective work further. Legacy & Long-Term Impact, Pillar 10, extends the question of what can continue beyond your direct involvement.

These teachings connect while keeping their own identities. You can complete the first action in this book with the skills, relationships and ordinary tools available to you now.

## Exercise: your next thirty days

Write a short commitment that fits your life:

> I will work with ______ on ______. My contribution is ______. We will review it on ______. I can offer ______ time. Before sharing anything, we will agree ______. At the review, we will decide ______.

Fill it in with the other participant. Keep a copy each of you can understand. If there is no willing participant yet, your next action is a listening conversation, not a fictional agreement.

Choose one person who can ask whether you followed through. Tell them the commitment without sharing somebody else's private information.

## Major Move: complete the exchange

Finish one contribution, review its usefulness and leave the next step clear. Then decide what you can repeat at a pace you can sustain.

That is your first practice of diaspora empowerment: recognizing ability, connecting it with a real need and increasing what people can do together.

## A million times

When I think about where I come from, I think about the struggle, stress and strife. I also think about the music, dancing, creativity and ingenuity that came through our lives.

Give me a million opportunities to change my upbringing and the people I come from, and I would choose my people a million times.

That is how I feel about it.

My confidence comes from what I have lived and what I have seen people carry. I want us to recognize that capacity in ourselves. I want us to be curious enough to look beyond the route everybody points toward and capable enough to make a way when we choose another turn.

Go left. Look carefully. Learn what is there. Build something useful with the people beside you.

Each one teach one.

Be happy, don't hurt anybody and move the culture forward.
